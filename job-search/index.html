How a ticket becomes a shipped design at Huma. Five steps, run with Claude Code and agents I built for it, and one rule behind all of them: nothing goes on screen unless I can point to the line that asks for it.

The business problem

Deals were everywhere, and nowhere

Promotions at Snappfood come in many forms: cashback, eco food, food packs, coupons, discounts. They exist to attract people who want to pay less. But they were fragmented across the app, so the people who cared most about them had the hardest time finding them.

Vendors couldn’t showcase offers

There was no place at the start of the journey to put a promotion in front of customers.

Promotions lacked impressions

Low visibility meant low engagement, so vendors saw little return on their offers.

Deal-seekers took too many steps

Users looking specifically for promotions couldn’t reach those vendors in a few taps.

Build different discovery journeys for different user segments, so each one finds the promotions it actually wants.

Prioritizing

Three ways in. We picked one.

We could grow the business impact of promotions in three ways. Customer experience was where the existing problems hurt most, and it would teach us how the system really behaved before we touched the vendor side.

01

More variety of promotions

Increase the kinds of offers available.

02

Better vendor tools

Improve promotion creation and reporting for vendors.

We started here03

More promotion use by customers

Help customers find and redeem promotions.

Data gathering

What the funnel told us

We started with a few questions. Do users understand what a promotion is worth? Is behavior consistent across promotion types? Does it matter how many promotions a user engages with, or how many get redeemed overall? To answer them, we analyzed user and business data along the product funnel.

Average order value rose about 3% in a year

It came from higher prices, not from changed behavior.

Some promotions lost money

Cashback in particular had become less profitable.

High cognitive load

Many promotion types meant more to process and longer loading times.

It looked like spam

Too many promotions with too little explanation felt like noise.

The old version

Three systems, three entry points

Two kinds: a percentage of the order credited back, or a fixed amount regardless of order size. Users met cashback in their profile (balance and history) and at checkout (what they’d earn).

Coupons unlock when a condition is met: free shipping over 890,000 tomans, 30% off a first order, a free side with a specific burger. They appeared on vendor cards, filters, menus and the basket. Users rarely filtered by coupon; most activated one from the menu once their basket qualified.

Parties run on four services (food, fruit, sweets and meat) and sit on Snappfood’s home page. They discount selected items, and drive about 4.7% of Snappfood’s sales.

Target users & metrics

Who it’s for, and how we’d know

Active cashback users

Know the benefits and actively look for lower spending.

Price-sensitive users

Search for discounts and promotions to save on every order.

Food Pack visitors

Already aware of Food Pack and come to learn what it offers.

  • Higher micro CR from discovery to menu for vendors with promotions.
  • Less time spent on discovery.
  • More use of displayed promotions: active cashback, discounted vendors, personal discounts, coupons, parties, buy-one-get-one.
  • Total micro CR from discovery to menu, starting at 79%.
  • Drop-off rate during discovery.
  • RPD (revenue per delivery) as a key indicator.

The risk we named up front: redirecting a big share of homepage traffic (for example, half of it) to a new page we didn’t yet understand could pull discovery-to-menu conversion down.

Hypothesis

A dedicated page for deal-seekers

Give price-sensitive users one discovery page that features vendors with promotions, and make its impressions and conversion match the home page and listings. The page needed three ways in.

A prominent place in the app’s navigation menu.

A dedicated section or filter inside the home page and vendor listings.

Promotional campaigns and notifications that drive awareness and bring people back.

Campaigns & notifications

Promotional campaigns and notifications point people to the Promotion Center to build awareness and engagement.

Design process

What others do with deals

We reviewed the promotion, reward and offer sections of competitors for first and last impressions, and for patterns that could work for single promotions and for bundles of products or vendors.

Borrowed from the App Store and Netflix

The App Store’s Today tab raised installs from browsing by showcasing the best apps every day as a feed. Netflix’s home screen and Previews drive discovery the same way. Both pointed at a dynamic, personal, content-led Promotion Center.

Search that understands what you’re after

Most people opened Balad to find a place, not to get directions. Search was the real front door of the app, and it wasn’t built for that job.

The world before

Every result looked the same

People came looking for something specific: a pharmacy open past nine, a restaurant nearby, a park they hadn’t been to yet. Under the routing sits a searchable map of Iran’s places, and that search layer was the actual reason the app got opened.

Yet every search returned the same generic layout. A pharmacy looked identical to a park, a restaurant no different from a repair shop. Nothing suggested the app understood what kind of place you wanted.

The everyday friction

Three kinds of friction

Search bounce rate was creeping up, because the app kept asking people to do work it should have done for them.

Dead ends

Search for something missing and the search just stopped. No suggestion, no alternative, no next move.

Detours to act

Tap a card, get pushed to a detail page, do the thing, then find your way back to browsing.

No memory

No idea what you searched last week, and no answer to the first question: is it open right now?

A rethink of how results look

“No results” had always been an edge case. I started treating it as a design problem in its own right.

The wider landscape

How others handle local search

Google Maps

Simplified what a result even looks like, betting that a cleaner map earns more trust than a denser one.

Citymapper

Treated discovery as something that should reward curiosity, tying places to a payoff instead of static text.

Neither was built for Farsi speakers or Iranian places. What we took from both: put the context people need into the result itself, and give them a reason to keep exploring after the first one.

The work

Components built for what you search

I rebuilt the search page around components tailored to the type of place, so a restaurant shows what a restaurant needs and a service shows what a service needs. Search history personalizes what surfaces first. Opening hours moved into the result itself.

No results

The “no results” screen now offers category browsing (gas station, restaurant, pharmacy and more), plus a way to add the place if it’s genuinely missing from the map.

BeforeAfter

Cards used to show information and nothing else. Navigate, Call and Save now sit right on the card, and a list-view toggle opens up browsing beyond the map.

BeforeAfter

Built for comparing two or three places

People kept weighing places against each other using single cards. The list view surfaces the details that decide it, like delivery, parking and restrooms, with one-tap call and navigate.

Introduction

Not another cloud drive

Vault is a specialized file sharing and management system. Its integration with Graph’s security products, especially the FAM line, makes sharing and managing files more secure and more collaborative inside the Graph ecosystem.

I worked on Vault from the start. Our goal was an interface that made organizing and sharing files across platforms simple.

The challenge

Files were the open door

Attacks on Iranian companies were rising, and unsafe files entering their systems were a major risk. Sanctions and security policy ruled out Google Drive and Dropbox. Graph’s FAM products handled file contamination, but managing files inside organizations still fell back on FTP and local tools. They needed an internal solution that was secure and compliant.

User-centric ideation & testing

Watching people work

As the only designer, I ran research with the marketing and support teams, then shaped the flows and interface. Two methods carried the research.

Contextual inquiry

Observing how people in organizations actually move, share and protect files during their working day.

Diary study

Tracking file tasks over time to catch the pain points that never show up in a single session.

What we learned shaped three principles:

Build on Graph’s existing user base

Graph already had customers and solid infrastructure. Linking Vault to other Graph products gave users one familiar, secure place to manage and share files.

Make it usable for everyone

Adoption depended on an interface that worked regardless of technical skill, so we worked to minimize complexity at every step.

Streamline data flow

Refining how data moves between Vault and Graph’s services removed disruptions and made file workflows faster.

The process

A redesign, without stopping the business

Stakeholders wanted a full redesign after research, but production constraints meant Graph’s current products had to keep selling. So I set up a five-step process for the new Vault team.

Keep Graph’s current selling products healthy while Vault is in development, fixing usability issues during the maintenance phase.

Define one primary persona to guide every design decision and address specific needs.

Restructure scenarios around the primary persona’s daily work, so interactions and workflows follow how they actually work.

Prototype new solutions for Vault’s information architecture to make files easier to find and act on.

Test the new solutions rigorously to validate them and catch problems before launch.

Designing the Vault

Benchmarks: Google Drive and Dropbox

We compared three areas: file discovery, sharing and management, and integrations. Google Drive and Dropbox set the bar for patterns and expectations.

Approach

Four phases, in order

Here’s how I take a design task from a blank page to a shippable UI.

Understand the client’s requirements, the target audience and the market, through interviews, surveys and research. Here: requirement analysis and competitor analysis.

Define goals, objectives and design strategy with stakeholders, aligning the product vision and key features. Here: personas and problem statements.

Create on-brand high-fidelity mockups and interactive prototypes, then iterate on feedback and usability testing. Here: UI design and design system.
