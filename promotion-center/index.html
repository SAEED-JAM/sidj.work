The Design Desk

Two designers, one product owner and about a hundred stories a quarter, for software used inside NHS GP practices. I built a desk of AI agents that reads the requirements, draws from the design system and checks the work. People answer the questions and approve.

Role
AI Design Engineer. I led the design track of version one and designed and built version two end to end.

The challenge

A hundred stories a quarter, and no room to guess

Huma builds UK Primary Care, software for NHS GP practices: the inbox where patient requests land, triage, messaging and appointments. In Q3 the product team wrote 33 PRDs with 266 numbered decisions and asked for 90 design tasks. Q4 added 18 more PRDs and 98 stories. Two designers drew all of it.

Speed was only half of it. In healthcare, a wireframe that invents behaviour can ship a rule nobody decided. It happened once in Q3: a control that could write to a patient’s consent record made it into a design, and was caught only because a developer asked what to store.

The other half was attention. Every open question either waited for the product owner or got answered quietly in a wireframe, where nobody would notice until it shipped.

Every state needs a source

A PRD line, a numbered decision or an acceptance criterion. A brief can rule something out. It can’t put something on screen.

Two design systems

Patient screens on the NHS design system, 390px first, WCAG 2.2 AA. Staff screens on a Material 3 kit at 1440px.

One person approves

The product owner’s attention is the bottleneck, so a question has to arrive researched, with options and a recommendation.

Version one · Q3 2026

A design loop inside git

The first version lived in the product team’s repo, next to the PRDs and decisions. Claude Code did the reading and the drawing through a set of skills and agents, and pull requests carried both the designs and the decisions.

My design track
  • The brief pack the first stories were drawn from
  • Before/after sheets: every state beside what it replaces, with its source
  • Handoff slices: one standalone file per story, every state one click away
  • Task audit: a ticket in, a change list out, each change citing its line
  • Handoff slicer: cuts a story’s screens out of the built prototype
The loop the team built around it
  • A desk that sent work to agents, each in its own git worktree
  • A decision helper that researched open questions and opened decision pull requests
  • A cold-read reviewer in a fresh context
  • A 14-check gate, lint scripts and CI on every design pull request

What broke

The cost moved from drawing to everything around it

By September the loop worked, and it was heavy. One batch review, nine stories over a week, counted 38 pull requests, about twenty worker agents, 23 worktrees left on disk and seven clashing question numbers. Its conclusion:

“Nothing in the cull touches what produced the designs; all of it touches what was spent checking, recording and renumbering around them.”

Decision log

Seven decisions for version two

I took those findings and redesigned the loop from scratch. Each decision gave something up.

01Git for knowing, canvases for drawing
Context
Designs in git meant pull requests for files nobody could review as a diff, plus worktrees and locks so agents wouldn’t collide.
Options
Keep everything in git · move everything into a design tool · split by job
Trade-off
Two places to keep straight, and design history that lives outside version control.
Outcome
PRDs, decisions and questions stay in git and change by pull request. Drawings live on Claude artifact canvases. Nothing designed goes to git.
02One record generates every handoff file
Context
Sheets and slices were authored separately and drifted.
Options
More checks on hand-made files · generate every file from one source
Trade-off
The record has to be strict. The generator refuses a state without a citation, a prop the surface doesn’t declare, or an open review item.
Outcome
A script with no model writes the cover, the slice, the before/after sheets, the notes and the state boards from one story record, then renders and checks every sheet.
03Check once, and look for what’s missing first
Context
Cold reads found nothing wrong, and the product owner’s findings were all absences.
Options
Keep repeat reads and the 14-check gate · one strong checker, run once
Trade-off
One pass has to count: a strong model, a fresh context, and no view of the drawer’s chat.
Outcome
Missing first, then invented, contradicted, drawn differently and off the design system.
04Cheap models read; strong models draw and judge
Context
Grounding a story means reading a lot. Drawing and checking need judgement.
Options
One strong model everywhere · a tier for each job
Trade-off
Agents hand work to each other, so every brief between them has to fit in ten lines.
Outcome
Researchers on a cheap model, the drawer and the checker on a strong one, the generator on none at all.
05Research every question before a person sees it
Context
The product owner’s attention was the bottleneck.
Options
Ask everything · decide in the wireframe · research first, then route
Trade-off
An undecided state ships as a labelled placeholder until someone answers.
Outcome
A decision helper settles what the sources already answer. Designer’s calls come with options and a recommendation. Only consent, safety, the patient record and billing go to the product owner, in one pull request per task.
06One workspace per story, promoted on approval
Context
Two designers, and many stories changing the same surfaces at once.
Options
One shared canvas · branches and locks · an isolated workspace per story
Trade-off
Copies to keep in step. A story built on another is brought up to date with a three-way merge, and a conflict stops for the designer.
Outcome
The product hub changes only on approval. Twenty of the first 37 stories were built on another story, up to five deep.
07Draw only from the design systems
Context
Hand-built parts drifted from the kit. As one review put it, correct and inconsistent is still a defect: a developer building it gets two components.
Options
Allow small hand-built parts · kit components only
Trade-off
A missing component blocks drawing until the design system has it.
Outcome
Kit component first, then patterns, then the shell, with no raw colour, spacing or type. A missing component becomes a finding for the design system.

Reflection

What building it taught me

01

Agents draw well and decide badly

So nothing in the system lets them decide. Undecided behaviour becomes a labelled placeholder and a question, never a guess.

02

Missing beats wrong

The expensive mistakes were absences: the empty state, the failure, the permission. Looking for them first changed what reviews found.

03

Make the record the product

Once one file held every state, citation and decision, the handoff files came for free and could never disagree.

04

Design the stops, not just the screens

The hard part was deciding where a person has to stop the line, and making each stop cheap: one question, with options.

The business problem

Deals were everywhere, and nowhere

Promotions at Snappfood come in many forms: cashback, eco food, food packs, coupons, discounts. They exist to attract people who want to pay less. But they were fragmented across the app, so the people who cared most about them had the hardest time finding them.

Vendors couldn’t showcase offers

There was no place at the start of the journey to put a promotion in front of customers.

Promotions lacked impressions

Low visibility meant low engagement, so vendors saw little return on their offers.

Deal-seekers took too many steps

Users looking specifically for promotions couldn’t reach those vendors in a few taps.

Build different discovery journeys for different user segments, so each one finds the promotions it actually wants.

Prioritizing

Three ways in. We picked one.

We could grow the business impact of promotions in three ways. Customer experience was where the existing problems hurt most, and it would teach us how the system really behaved before we touched the vendor side.

01

More variety of promotions

Increase the kinds of offers available.

02

Better vendor tools

Improve promotion creation and reporting for vendors.

Data gathering

What the funnel told us

We started with a few questions. Do users understand what a promotion is worth? Is behavior consistent across promotion types? Does it matter how many promotions a user engages with, or how many get redeemed overall? To answer them, we analyzed user and business data along the product funnel.

Average order value rose about 3% in a year

It came from higher prices, not from changed behavior.

Some promotions lost money

Cashback in particular had become less profitable.

High cognitive load

Many promotion types meant more to process and longer loading times.

It looked like spam

Too many promotions with too little explanation felt like noise.

The old version

Three systems, three entry points

Two kinds: a percentage of the order credited back, or a fixed amount regardless of order size. Users met cashback in their profile (balance and history) and at checkout (what they’d earn).

Coupons unlock when a condition is met: free shipping over 890,000 tomans, 30% off a first order, a free side with a specific burger. They appeared on vendor cards, filters, menus and the basket. Users rarely filtered by coupon; most activated one from the menu once their basket qualified.

Parties run on four services (food, fruit, sweets and meat) and sit on Snappfood’s home page. They discount selected items, and drive about 4.7% of Snappfood’s sales.

The risk we named up front: redirecting a big share of homepage traffic (for example, half of it) to a new page we didn’t yet understand could pull discovery-to-menu conversion down.

Hypothesis

A dedicated page for deal-seekers

Give price-sensitive users one discovery page that features vendors with promotions, and make its impressions and conversion match the home page and listings. The page needed three ways in.

A prominent place in the app’s navigation menu.

A dedicated section or filter inside the home page and vendor listings.

Promotional campaigns and notifications that drive awareness and bring people back.

Campaigns & notifications

Promotional campaigns and notifications point people to the Promotion Center to build awareness and engagement.

Design process

What others do with deals

We reviewed the promotion, reward and offer sections of competitors for first and last impressions, and for patterns that could work for single promotions and for bundles of products or vendors.

Borrowed from the App Store and Netflix

The App Store’s Today tab raised installs from browsing by showcasing the best apps every day as a feed. Netflix’s home screen and Previews drive discovery the same way. Both pointed at a dynamic, personal, content-led Promotion Center.

The world before

Every result looked the same

How others handle local search

Simplified what a result even looks like, betting that a cleaner map earns more trust than a denser one.

Introduction

Not another cloud drive

Vault is a specialized file sharing and management system. Its integration with Graph’s security products, especially the FAM line, makes sharing and managing files more secure and more collaborative inside the Graph ecosystem.

I worked on Vault from the start. Our goal was an interface that made organizing and sharing files across platforms simple.

The challenge

Files were the open door

Attacks on Iranian companies were rising, and unsafe files entering their systems were a major risk. Sanctions and security policy ruled out Google Drive and Dropbox. Graph’s FAM products handled file contamination, but managing files inside organizations still fell back on FTP and local tools. They needed an internal solution that was secure and compliant.

User-centric ideation & testing

Watching people work

As the only designer, I ran research with the marketing and support teams, then shaped the flows and interface. Two methods carried the research.

Contextual inquiry

Observing how people in organizations actually move, share and protect files during their working day.

Diary study

Tracking file tasks over time to catch the pain points that never show up in a single session.

What we learned shaped three principles:

Build on Graph’s existing user base

Graph already had customers and solid infrastructure. Linking Vault to other Graph products gave users one familiar, secure place to manage and share files.

Make it usable for everyone

Adoption depended on an interface that worked regardless of technical skill, so we worked to minimize complexity at every step.

Streamline data flow

Refining how data moves between Vault and Graph’s services removed disruptions and made file workflows faster.

The process

A redesign, without stopping the business

Stakeholders wanted a full redesign after research, but production constraints meant Graph’s current products had to keep selling. So I set up a five-step process for the new Vault team.

Keep Graph’s current selling products healthy while Vault is in development, fixing usability issues during the maintenance phase.

Define one primary persona to guide every design decision and address specific needs.

Restructure scenarios around the primary persona’s daily work, so interactions and workflows follow how they actually work.

Prototype new solutions for Vault’s information architecture to make files easier to find and act on.

Test the new solutions rigorously to validate them and catch problems before launch.

Designing the Vault

Benchmarks: Google Drive and Dropbox

We compared three areas: file discovery, sharing and management, and integrations. Google Drive and Dropbox set the bar for patterns and expectations.
